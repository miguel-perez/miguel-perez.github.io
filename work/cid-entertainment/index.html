A hotel-booking startup learns what festival fans actually needed.

CID Entertainment sold VIP packages for music festivals and had built Curadora, a site that found fans a hotel near the event. I joined in July 2016 to define how it would enter the festival-fan market. We put a metric on the roadmap, tested its first assumption with ten customers, and found the hotel was not the pain. Two more rounds asked fans what was: going alone, and planning a trip for a group. The roadmap turned toward the group.

Outline

Four months to find out what festival fans needed from a hotel-booking startup, and to leave the team a way to keep asking.

{% include study-outline.html %}
{% include study-act.html n=1 %}

CID Entertainment sells VIP packages and travel for music festivals. In 2016 it had a second product, Curadora, a booking site that found fans a hotel near the event. A festival would make Curadora its official hotel partner. The site listed hundreds of hotels by distance from the venue, with shuttle routes and a link to tickets. An outside team had built it over the previous year. I joined in July as its product designer, with a brief to define how it would enter the festival-fan market.

The first evening was a workshop with the whole team. We wrote the pitch as a fill-in-the-blanks sentence: for whom, with what need, unlike whom. We demoed competitors over dinner. Then we mapped a fan's journey in five columns, from hearing about an event to the week after it. In each column we wrote what the fan sees, asks, enjoys, and hates, and where Curadora could help. Every note came from us. Nobody in the room had asked a fan yet. The open questions on the map showed it: why not just use a big travel site? Are my friends going?

August went to the product as it stood. The event page put the hotel search above the festival's own banner, so a fan landed on a form before seeing where they were going. I moved the search bar under the festival banner and made the event header a piece the outside team could reuse. I added a link to manage a booking, in the header and on the error pages. They were small changes, shipped through the outside team's codebase and tests. After them, the first thing a fan saw was the festival.

Seven weeks in, on 13 September, a larger company in the same business bought CID. The work carried on with a new owner to explain it to. Two days later the CEO put a question to the team that I wrote down: how would we know we were growing, where was the bus going, and when would we celebrate? Revenue was the obvious answer, and for a product this early it read as a flat line. A roadmap was the other answer, and celebrating each feature we shipped would tell us nothing about whether anyone wanted it.

I proposed one metric instead: traction, the rate at which we turned people who had never heard of us into happy customers. I drew it as a customer factory. People are acquired, activated, and pay. Some come back, and some refer a friend, who enters at the top. The number to watch was throughput, new customers a day. For 2016 the goal was market entry, which meant finding a problem fans had that we could solve. Growth and scale could wait until we had.

To run it, we set up an experiment board that treated the business model like software. Each strategy had a version number. Each experiment under it was a card with a hypothesis that could fail, a way to run it, and space for the result and what we learned next. The first strategy, version 1.0, was to sell hotels for events we did not partner with. Its first experiment was ten interviews.

{% include study-act.html n=3 %}

The hypothesis was specific. Curadora solved two problems: finding a place to stay near the event, and getting around once there. If we interviewed ten recent customers about them, eight would agree to a follow-up and two would refer a friend. I wrote the script and the note-taking guide, emailed customers who had booked in the last month, and ran the ten calls myself. Six agreed to keep talking. Nobody referred anyone. Fans told us they found hotels with a map app or a big travel site in minutes and got around by rideshare, shuttle, or their own car. The one person who struggled to place a hotel ran an operations crew that worked events rather than attending them. The product fit that one person, and that person was not a fan.

That left two moves: keep the product and change the customer, or keep the customer and change the product. The company's vision was fans, and the CEO chose them. So the second round tested two different problems, which the ten had raised between our questions. Going to a festival alone is no fun. Planning a trip for a group takes work. Thirteen fans, recruited through friends and past customers, took the same twenty-minute call with the new script. Twelve agreed to keep talking and four referred someone. The pains that came up most were finding friends to go with, sorting out the logistics before the trip, and keeping everyone coordinated during it.

The third round was planned as ten more interviews, with each fan ranking the three pains. Referrals and outreach together filled the board faster than we could schedule: every call ended with a request for an introduction and a paragraph the fan could paste to a friend. It ran as a funnel in Trello; the animation at the top of this page is that recruiting board. Each person was a card, moved from lead to scheduled to interviewed, with a lane for no-shows. Two more lanes held the people to follow up with and the people retained, the report's word for a fan who agreed to keep talking. Each card carried a label for everything the person told us, from age band and budget to whether they camp. In twelve days it held 127 cards, twelve of them by referral, and we interviewed 36 people instead of 10.

Thirty-six calls were more than one person could run in the time. Three teammates from guest services, ticketing, and project management took interviews on my script. They typed their notes into a shared form with the same five columns for everyone: who the person is, how they discover events, how they decide, how they plan, and what happens on the day. We had set it up so it could run without me in the room.

The notes became a customer theory. In Trello I built the journey map as a theory board the team could add to. Three personas sat at the left. Then came seven stages, from discovery to long after the event, each holding insight cards written in the first person. Every card listed what the fan was trying to do, what they hoped for, and what got in the way. It gave how many of the 56 people on the board had said it and how confident we were. Where one existed, it linked a published paper that said the same thing. Anyone could open a stage, read the evidence, and add to it.

To find the personas, I clustered the labels. I ran 48 tags, from age bands to habits, through a clustering routine in R that groups tags that travel together. The tree split cleanly in two. One side was fans who value convenience: older, in small groups, first-timers or occasional, hotel over tent, willing to spend. The other was the planners: younger, in groups of three or more, camping, budget-conscious, splitting every cost with a core group of friends. Those two sides became the board's festie and non-camper. The planner, the friend who runs the trip, was the third persona, and its tag sat on the festie side. The tag for Curadora's own customers sat on the convenience side. Curadora had never talked to a planner.

By November the research said that buying a hotel was a small part of a fan's trip. The roadmap we had inherited had been written without asking one. I proposed three days to act on it, and wrote up why: building from what fans told us was less risky than building what we had guessed. The product lead agreed, and I facilitated it with the team that had run the interviews.

Day one turned the insights into opportunities. We told the fans' stories back to the room, wrote questions that start with how might we, under eight headings from forming groups to splitting payments, and dot-voted. The business side then picked a direction with rough estimates of upside and effort. Day two was solutions. Teammates demoed eighteen sites that each solved part of the problem. Then everyone sketched alone: eight ideas in eight minutes, and one finished sketch with real words on it and no placeholder text. Day three was critique: sketches on a wall, silent votes, three minutes of discussion each, then a straw poll and the product lead's decision.

The sketches were not hotel features. One had a bot ping a fan that friends nearby were planning a road trip, then let them join or start a car pool with a set contribution. Another was a planning assistant. Others were group savings, split payments, and a shared packing list. We merged the ideas into eighteen feature themes, and four of us scored each for upside, downside, urgency, and risk by planning poker, scoring in private and then comparing. Payment plans, group collaboration tools, group savings, and a planning wizard tied at the top for upside. The bot and the crowdfunding idea scored highest for risk.

The next step was to map the assumptions under the four winning themes and test them after Thanksgiving. My role ended in November, in the transition after the acquisition, with the theory, the funnel, and the scripts in the team's hands.

Afterwards

{{ page.after }}

  • The interview practice. Scripts, note-taking guides, the outreach and referral emails, and a recruiting board the team ran in the third round. Three teammates took the third round's notes on the same template, and the theory board was built to be added to.
  • A roadmap pointed at fans. The feature list moved from booking a hotel toward how fans travel: in groups, around a planner, splitting costs. The four themes at the top of the sheet were group collaboration tools, payment plans, group savings, and a planning wizard.
  • Experiment cards. Every idea after September had to be written as a card with a hypothesis that could fail, and the first one did.
