A snack-box homepage, redesigned across three time zones and measured.

Love With Food sold monthly boxes of snacks and donated a meal for every box. Its site was built for desktops while its traffic moved to phones, and its team was spread from Philadelphia to Manila with a few shared hours a day.

In a year we interviewed nine members, ran a design sprint by video, redrew the homepage six times, built it as components, and measured it. Sign-ups rose 60% by A/B test. On the account pages that followed, eight of eight testers completed the top tasks that fewer than a third had before.

Outline

A year to move a snack-box homepage from desktops to phones, and to leave the team a measured component library.

{% include study-outline.html %}
{% include study-act.html n=1 %}

Love With Food sold a monthly box of snacks and donated a meal to a food bank for each box. Its site had been built for desktops while its traffic was moving to phones. The CEO asked for a responsive redesign, and we agreed on one goal: a better experience on phones, and a homepage that told new visitors what membership gave them.

The team was in California, the rest of the United States, and Asia, and we shared only a few hours a day. Our daily stand-up ran at 22:00 GMT on the timetable: 6:00 pm in Philadelphia, 3:00 pm in San Francisco, and 6:00 am in Manila and Nanjing. It took fifteen minutes, every day but Friday. Sprint planning, review, and retrospective sat at 22:15 GMT on a two-week cycle.

Instead of an in-person kickoff, I recorded interviews with our subject-matter experts, cut the highlights, and put them in a private YouTube playlist. That playlist was the brief before ideation.

{% include study-methods.html n=1 %}
{% include study-act.html n=2 %}

The CEO wanted personas. I wanted them built on evidence, and I wanted to do it remotely. So we took two steps: a questionnaire, then interviews. The questionnaire went to existing and new members and asked what had motivated them to sign up. The interviews were recruited from its answers.

We coded the responses into more than a hundred attributes per person: age band, household, income band, habits, media, shopping, food, and attitudes to the service. Then we correlated and factored the attributes, and two segments came out.

One segment was younger, urban, employed, with no children, price-aware, snacking more than three times a day, buying snacks online, and hearing about the service from a friend. The other was 30 to 45, suburban, a stay-at-home parent with children, cooking at home, reading labels, using the box to discover food, treating opening it as a ritual, and buying it as a gift.

I interviewed nine members over Google Hangouts, one 30-minute video each. Three teammates and I coded the videos through a shared form. They coded seven, seven, and five videos and I coded two, so most videos were coded by two or three people. Then I compared the interviews with one another.

The interviews added what the survey could not. People who signed up for a steady flow of snacks also liked introducing coworkers to new ones. Parents who used the box to discover snacks bought the ones they liked locally when they saw them. Not knowing what was in the box worried some people at first. Later it was the part they looked forward to.

We wrote persona candidates and reviewed them in a silent critique: ten minutes of silent reading, comments only on what worked, two red votes each, with the CEO holding four. Then marketing read the report. Their social media work targeted a segment that sat in neither persona. I helped them define a third, proto-persona and added it rather than pushing back. Looking back on it in print, I put the miss on myself:

If I had spent more time up-front capturing existing assumptions, perhaps by starting with proto-personas, I could have avoided having to supplement the report later.

The homepage ran as a remote design sprint. It opened with an overview by video, thirty-five minutes a person: a short meeting, then the highlight videos, with each person noting what jumped out. Then each of us diverged alone for fifty minutes: a mind map, a crazy-eights warm-up of eight quick sketches, and a storyboard of a user flow. Videos I recorded of myself walking through the exercises guided each step. Everyone scanned their sketches and sent them to me, and I put them on the whiteboard without names.

Review took forty minutes. First a silent critique: blue soft votes on any design or part of one, as many as each person wanted, two or three on a favorite. Then three minutes of group comments per design, with the author adding notes last. Then super-voting: one red vote each on the strongest design, with the CEO holding three and me holding two.

Then the decide step, a private meeting where I listed the conflicts and assumptions so the CEO could choose one direction or more than one. Then the user story: one day for me to draw a detailed sketch stringing the flow together. Then the prototype: one day for the graphic designer and me to build a click-through in InVision. Then validation: five unmoderated sessions of the mobile prototype, everyone watching and noting the good, the bad, and the interesting. One note mattered most. The page said our mission, but it never said, in one sentence, that this was a subscription that sends you snacks.

The homepage moved from a hand-drawn wireframe through six drawn generations. The hero went from a photo of the box on a table, to a grid of snacks, to text alone, to a photo of a member with the box. The benefits went from a row of three icons to four dark tiles to photo tiles. Last month's box gave way to a band counting the meals donated, and the shop's products gained tabs and a member price.

Two five-second tests on Mechanical Turk measured whether the page said what it was. The same three questions ran each time: what product or service is provided, who is the company, and what is the benefit to you. Generation 1 ran 1 to 5 June with 17 responses: 13 named the company, 11 of them exactly, and 14 described a snack box or a delivery. None said junk-free, and none mentioned the mission. Generation 2 ran 12 June with 58 responses: 31 named the company, 19 of them exactly, and 32 described a snack box or a delivery. Eleven said junk-free, 4 mentioned feeding the hungry, and 14 left the benefit blank or unsure. After the second round we settled on one direction. In print, I put the delay on myself:

We could have moved faster had I been more attuned to the internal politics that ultimately decided the outcome of the solution.

I broke the new designs into reusable components and built them in Handlebars as a living style guide. Each component carried its alternative options and named the pieces it depended on. The build ran at five breakpoints: desktop, iPad, iPad mini, iPhone 6 Plus, and iPhone 5. A responsive build pipeline and a versioned image and font pipeline synced to S3 carried it.

Then we measured. Without metrics it is hard to know when a design is good enough, so each major change ran as an A/B test with a hypothesis card. One card is redrawn below: remove the Learn more button, and look for a significant rise in sign-ups.

Together, the first few major changes led to a 60% lift in sign-up conversions, measured by A/B test. The style guide let the engineers redesign other parts of the site quickly, and the blog, on a different back end, consumed the same components.

Customer service was fielding a large share of its calls about account details, so the account pages went to the top of the roadmap. We voted on what members came to do. Subscribe and shop led with 10 votes each. Change plan and update addresses had 6 each. Understand the service and write reviews had 5 each, and cancel, refer a friend, and past orders had 3 each.

My employment ended in November 2015, and the account work continued as a contract into 2016. In January I ran three one-person pilot studies on UserTesting to shake out the protocol, covering account services, subscription, and the snack shop. In February we ran two five-person rounds on the existing account pages. Finding when and where the next box arrives hit a problem for 3 of 5, and so did changing the shipping address. Changing the billing card tripped 2 of 5, finding the membership settings 2 of 5, and signing in none.

On the SUPR-Q, a standard questionnaire scored against other sites, the existing pages sat at the 18th percentile for usability, 24th for trust, 45th for loyalty, and 29th for appearance. Unmoderated studies had already shown top tasks like updating a shipping address completing below 30%.

The redesign was one account area. On desktop a rail listed profile and billing, reviews, referrals, points, subscription plans, order history, and loved items. On the phone the same pages sat under a Your account bar with a menu: seven desktop screens and nine phone screens. When we tested the redesigned pages, 8 of 8 participants completed the top tasks.

Afterwards

{{ page.after }}

Love With Food later closed. Its site survives in the Internet Archive: the homepage before the redesign, captured 2 March 2015, the homepage after, captured 5 June 2015, and captures through November 2016.

Read the homepage before, Internet Archive, 2 March 2015 and the homepage after, 5 June 2015.

In 2026 I went back to the archived pages to re-score the 2015 tasks with a newer instrument, and I could not. The account tasks sat behind a sign-in the archive never captured. The archive shows the pages, and the numbers stand as they were published.

What stayed was the component library and the style guide the engineers kept building on, and the method, which I wrote up in 2019.
