I · Avalere Health · 2017

One designer, no users

Avalere Health is a healthcare policy and data firm in Washington, DC, and since 2015 an Inovalon company. Its Philadelphia software team had shipped a market-analytics product and was chasing feature requests, with a design vendor billing per sprint. In July 2017 I joined as the first UX designer. Nobody on the team had direct access to users.

So the first job was not a screen. It was finding one low-risk study that leaders would let me run, and using the readout to explain what user research is and how it differs from market research.

The intranet study

The team rebuilding the company intranet had no researcher, so I offered. Intranet users were our own staff, which made recruiting easy. We ran a Mural workshop to agree what staff needed to get done, then watched five colleagues attempt ten tasks each.

The readout went to HR leadership as a teaching document: what we did, what broke, and what to change first. Finding a specific role inside a department failed for three of five people, so the first recommendation was to put department names on the org chart. HR became the first ally. Their agreement that the organisation needed to grow its design capability was the seed of everything after.

Swarms, not triangles

Alongside the study we ran lunch-and-learns on how to think like a designer, built on the company's own imagery. Avalere's logo is a flock of birds. Geese fly in a triangle behind one leader, which works when the route is known. Starlings swarm, and a swarm follows three rules: alignment, separation, cohesion. Complex problems need the swarm.

The three rules became the three moves of the deck: find alignment through experimentation, facilitate collaboration among separate stakeholders, and tell a cohesive story about human needs. We tested the framing on analysts and product managers before running it on site. The marketing team came on as the second ally.

METHODS · task-based usability study, completion and error measures (Sauro & Lewis) · workshop facilitation (Schwarz) · tame versus wicked problems (Rittel & Webber) · values-based framing
II · Avalere Health · late 2017

Into the sales call

The market-analytics product was a year past its first release. A market study had been commissioned at the start, so the team felt the research was done. Prospects kept asking for one more feature before they would buy. That is the shape of a build trap.

I joined the demos and interviewed fifteen stakeholders to pull out the assumptions behind the product and its business model. The buyers were busy executives, and the company wanted a finished product in the room to protect its brand. We proposed the opposite: ask before you demo. Sales agreed to have us in the room as the inquisitive product person.

Prospects turned out to be glad to talk about their own workflows. The conversation moved from the demo to the problems Avalere was uniquely placed to solve, contract sizes grew, and a new group formed to find repeatable services that could become products. By mid-2018 the product page had come down from the public site.

METHODS · customer discovery (Blank & Dorf) · assumption mapping · solution interviews and the FOCUS ladder (Garbugli, Lean B2B) · service blueprint (Shostack)
III · Inovalon · 2018

Bringing design in-house

In January 2018 a reorganisation moved me to the Inovalon parent, with a promotion, and in June a second designer joined, a design researcher. Together we set out to understand about a dozen product teams and to move the work being outsourced to a design agency back in-house.

We interviewed managers and tech leads for each product. They liked the agency: high-fidelity mockups, fast and consistent. What they missed was anyone responsible for the whole client experience and how it tied back to company measures. Analysts wrote requirements, the agency drew screens, and nobody in between asked why.

We ran the Innovator's Compass with each team in Mural to define workstreams and write testable hypotheses, and we kept the findings in an atomic research repository. Every two weeks we ran two or three experiments. Data became observations, observations became insights, insights became scenarios, and scenarios became solutions.

The transition, in ten steps

Moving the vendor's work in-house without a gap took most of 2018. The order mattered: we could not write a business case before we knew the costs, and we could not know the costs before we knew the work.

  1. Access, onboarding, and compliance
  2. Stakeholder interviews across the products
  3. Backlog creation and prioritisation
  4. Transition alignment with the vendor
  5. Design support costs and equipment list
  6. Job descriptions and compensation analysis with HR
  7. The design team business case
  8. Transfer of the design assets
  9. Component library contribution and bug guidelines
  10. Component library 1.0 release

The business case

In September 2018 the case went to leadership with the numbers attached. The vendor's contract ran to $687k for 2018. Four in-house designers replaced it. Total design spend fell 26% in the 2019 plan while delivery capacity doubled, and the Design Center of Excellence became an official corporate goal with approved headcount: an AVP of Design and a remote designer.

The budget model was formula-linked, so leadership could change an assumption and watch the totals move. Three scenarios were on the table. Try them.

METHODS · Innovator's Compass (Ben-Ur) · hypothesis statements and proto-personas (Gothelf & Seiden, Lean UX) · atomic research repository · boundary objects (Star & Griesemer)
IV · Inovalon · 2018

Proving it pays

Inovalon had launched NLPaaS in April 2018, a service that reads medical records and finds conditions a chart review would otherwise miss. Prospects wanted a way to review the findings themselves, and the team came to us with a large budget and a deadline: a working interface for Client Congress, the annual client conference, at the end of September.

I proposed spending a tenth of the budget on a high-fidelity prototype instead of production code, because a click-through changes faster than software while we are still learning. We captured the team's assumptions in a business model canvas, drew proto-personas and a twelve-frame storyboard, and cut the demo to the flows that mattered.

We had assumed reviewers would accept a finding above a confidence threshold. Then we watched clinical reviewers work, and the first session refuted it. They did not trust an automated finding they could not check against the chart, and my contextual inquiry notes say so in their words. The interface was rethought around evidence: every suggested condition points to the page and the sentence that supports it, and reviewers can capture what the model missed. The machine-learning threshold slider went. So did the mandatory reason for rejection.

The demo video played without a hitch at Client Congress. The team came home with a list of prospects and leadership funded a group of developers to build it. Over two years, discovery work like this avoided roughly $2.4M in rewrites, by the practice's own estimate of rework not built: about a tenth of a build budget spent on learning, before the other nine tenths were committed. A competitor moved its product into the reviewer's workflow the following year, which I read as convergence rather than cause.

METHODS · minimum viable product and innovation accounting (Ries) · context scenarios (Cooper) · sketching before building (Buxton) · contextual inquiry · the ironies of automation (Bainbridge)
V · Inovalon · 2019

Scaling it

After the ABILITY Network acquisition in 2018, Inovalon had dozens of development teams across four business units, and two designers to serve them. We could not be the bottleneck. The strategy became growing practitioners inside the teams: a Design Center of Excellence that trains, mentors, and certifies people who already do the work, and lets teams decide when to hire a designer.

One empathy map on a skeptical stakeholder set that direction. The analysts already had the domain knowledge. What they lacked was method. So we would train them.

We kicked off in March 2019 with founding practitioners from every business unit and a design-ops canvas to agree what the centre does, for whom, and what constrains it. Then we measured where the organisation stood.

Product and development managers already spent about a fifth of their time on design. Thirteen people spent more than half, and they scored around three times the average maturity. Combined with a matrix of every product's stage, revenue, and receptiveness, that found the first cohort of twenty.

Manager interviews told us what the cohort needed: how to write a testable hypothesis, how to design an experiment, how to recruit users. So the programme was blueprinted like an internal startup, with the same five measures a startup would track. We wrote the measures before the first cohort, so the programme could fail visibly rather than quietly.

LUMA Institute would deliver two of the three training days and the playbook. Userlytics was chosen because it records and redacts protected health information, which is what let studies run under HIPAA. We joined Glean.ly's alpha partner programme for the repository. A pilot cohort of six was set for September 2019 and the first cohort of twenty for October, then one a quarter toward a hundred practitioners leading research.

A manager had once said Inovalon was not the kind of place designers would want to work. The job postings drew more than 200 qualified applicants in two months. I left in July 2019 with the core team hired and the first cohort on the calendar. A hundred birds, and the route left to them.

METHODS · design maturity model (DMI) · double-loop learning (Argyris) · situated learning and communities of practice (Lave & Wenger) · pirate metrics (McClure; Croll & Yoskovitz) · service blueprint · ladder of participation (Arnstein)
Afterwards
