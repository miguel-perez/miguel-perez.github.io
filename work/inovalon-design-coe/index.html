The situation

Avalere Health is a healthcare policy and data firm. In 2017 it had no UX function, no direct access to users, and a design vendor billing $27,136 per sprint. Human-centered design was a new idea there, and most product work ran through an Agile process that had never had a designer in it.

I joined as the first UX designer. The job, as it turned out, was to make research something the company would pay for, act on, and eventually run on its own.

One study for HR

The intranet-redesign team had no researcher, so I made myself available. We ran Avalere's first rigorous usability study with internal employees, using a Mural workshop to define the task scenarios. The readout was written for HR leadership and did two jobs at once: it reported the findings, and it taught the difference between user research and market research.

HR became the first ally. Their agreement was the seed of everything that followed.

Alongside it, I ran lunch-and-learns built on the company's own values. Avalere's name comes from birds flocking, so swarms versus triangles became the metaphor for design on wicked problems. I tested the framing on business analysts and product managers before running it on site, and the marketing team came on as a second ally.

The pivot to training

The first draft of the strategy replaced outsourced analysts with in-house designers. One empathy map on a skeptical stakeholder changed that. The people already doing analysis had the domain knowledge; what they lacked was method. So the strategy became training them, and that decision is where the cohort model comes from.

A DMI-based design-maturity survey, 100+ responses across four business units, found the founding cohort: 20 practitioners who were already leading research informally.

The business case

In September 2018 the case went to leadership with the numbers attached. The vendor contract ran $27,136 per sprint, $687k for the year. Four in-house designers at $202k fully loaded each replaced it. Total design spend fell 26% while delivery capacity doubled, and the Design Center of Excellence became an official corporate goal with approved headcount: an AVP of Design and a remote designer.

The budget model was formula-linked, so leadership could change an assumption and watch the totals move.

What we built to run it

  • Design system. Built in React, documented in Sketch, version-controlled in Abstract with a git-pinned version, with front-end governance around it.
  • Research stack. An Airtable atomic-research repository tracking workstreams, stakeholders, and experiments across ten dimensions. Glean.ly as an alpha partner, Userlytics as a PHI-redacting platform, LUMA Institute as delivery partner.
  • Compliance. De-identification built into the process, so studies could run on protected health information under HIPAA.
  • Hiring. Job descriptions and a compensation analysis with HR. A manager had said designers wouldn't want to work there; the postings drew 200+ qualified applicants in two months.
  • Certification. 20 practitioners per cohort, quarterly from Q4 2019, toward 100 actively leading research, with design-tribe rituals and internal case studies to capture economic impact.

What it changed

Two years of discovery research and strategic design avoided roughly $2.4M in code rewrites. One example: an NLP prototype built for about 10% of the production budget shipped for Client Congress in three months. A contextual inquiry along the way found that clinicians did not fully trust automated tools, so the product was repositioned as an enabler before it was built. Months later its main competitor announced a redesign that closely matched the approach.

Market Navigator had spent two years on external market research alone. Capturing the assumptions and testing them with clients made the pivot obvious to leadership. Contract sizes grew, and a new department formed to find repeatable services that could become products.
