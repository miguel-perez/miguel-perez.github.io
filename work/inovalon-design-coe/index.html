I · Avalere Health · 2017

One designer, no users

Avalere Health is a healthcare policy and data firm in Washington, DC, owned by Inovalon since 2015. Its Philadelphia software team had shipped a market-analytics product and was chasing feature requests, with a design vendor billing per sprint. I joined in July 2017 as the first UX designer. Nobody on the team had ever watched a user.

So the first job was not a screen. It was one low-risk study that leaders would let me run, and a readout that showed what user research is.

The intranet study

The team rebuilding the company intranet had no researcher, so I offered. The users were our own staff, so recruiting was easy. A Mural workshop agreed what staff needed to get done; then we watched five colleagues attempt ten tasks each.

The readout went to HR leadership as a teaching document: what we did, what broke, what to fix first. Finding a role inside a department failed for three of five people, so the first recommendation was department names on the org chart. HR became the first ally, and their agreement that the company needed to grow its design capability was the seed of everything after.

Swarms, not triangles

Alongside the study we ran lunch-and-learns on thinking like a designer, built on the company's own imagery. Avalere's logo is a flock of birds. Geese fly in a triangle behind one leader, which works when the route is known. Starlings swarm, following three rules: alignment, separation, cohesion. Complex problems need the swarm.

The rules became the deck's three moves: find alignment through experimentation, facilitate collaboration among separate stakeholders, and tell a cohesive story about human needs. Marketing came on as the second ally.

II · Avalere Health · late 2017

Into the sales call

The market-analytics product was a year past its first release. A market study had been commissioned at the start, so the team felt the research was done. Prospects kept asking for one more feature before they would buy: the shape of a build trap.

I joined the demos and interviewed fifteen stakeholders to surface the assumptions behind the product and its business model. The buyers were busy executives, and the company wanted a finished product in the room. We proposed the opposite: ask before you demo. Sales let us in as the inquisitive product person.

Prospects were glad to talk about their own workflows. The conversation moved from the demo to the problems Avalere was uniquely placed to solve, contract sizes grew, and a new group formed to find repeatable services that could become products. By mid-2018 the product page had come down from the public site.

III · Inovalon · 2018

Bringing design in-house

In January 2018 a reorganisation moved me to the Inovalon parent, with a promotion. In June a design researcher joined. Together we set out to understand about a dozen product teams and bring the work outsourced to a design agency back in-house.

Managers and tech leads liked the agency: high-fidelity mockups, fast and consistent. What they missed was anyone responsible for the whole client experience and how it tied back to company measures. Analysts wrote requirements, the agency drew screens, and nobody in between asked why.

We ran the Innovator's Compass with each team to define workstreams and write testable hypotheses, and kept the findings in an atomic research repository. Every two weeks we ran two or three experiments. Data became observations, observations insights, insights scenarios, and scenarios solutions.

The transition, in ten steps

Moving the vendor's work in-house without a gap took most of 2018. The order mattered: no business case before we knew the costs, and no costs before we knew the work.

  1. Access, onboarding, and compliance
  2. Stakeholder interviews across the products
  3. Backlog creation and prioritisation
  4. Transition alignment with the vendor
  5. Design support costs and equipment list
  6. Job descriptions and compensation analysis with HR
  7. The design team business case
  8. Transfer of the design assets
  9. Component library contribution and bug guidelines
  10. Component library 1.0 release

The business case

In September 2018 the case went to leadership with the workbook attached. The design vendor billed $27,136 a sprint, $687k across 2018, for a five-role team. Four in-house designers replaced it: total design spend fell 26% in the 2019 plan while the model's delivery capacity doubled. The Design Center of Excellence became a corporate goal with approved headcount, an AVP of Design and a remote designer.

The budget model ran to eleven formula-linked sheets, so leadership could change an assumption and watch the totals move. This is the sheet they decided on.

IV · Inovalon · 2018

Proving it pays

In April 2018 Inovalon had launched a service that reads medical records and finds conditions a chart review would miss. Prospects wanted to review the findings themselves, and the team came to us with a large budget and a deadline: a working interface for Client Congress, the annual client conference, at the end of September.

I proposed spending a tenth of the budget on a high-fidelity prototype instead of production code, because a click-through changes faster than software while we are still learning. We captured the team's assumptions in a business model canvas, drew proto-personas and a twelve-frame storyboard, and cut the demo to the flows that mattered.

We had assumed reviewers would accept any finding above a confidence threshold. The first session with clinical reviewers refuted it: they would not trust a finding they could not check against the chart. So the interface was rethought around evidence. Every suggested condition points to the page and sentence that supports it, and reviewers can capture what the model missed. The threshold slider went, and so did the mandatory reason for rejection.

The demo played without a hitch at Client Congress. The team came home with a list of prospects, and leadership funded a group of developers to build it. Over two years, discovery work like this avoided an estimated $2.4M in rewrites: a tenth of a build budget spent on learning before the other nine tenths were committed.

V · Inovalon · 2019

Scaling it

After the ABILITY Network acquisition in 2018, Inovalon had dozens of development teams across four business units, and two designers to serve them. We could not be the bottleneck. The strategy became growing practitioners inside the teams: a Design Center of Excellence that trains, mentors, and certifies people who already do the work, and lets teams decide when to hire a designer.

One empathy map on a skeptical stakeholder set the direction. The analysts had the domain knowledge; what they lacked was method. So we would train them.

We kicked off in March 2019 with founding practitioners from every business unit and a design-ops canvas: what the centre does, for whom, and within what limits. Then we measured where the organisation stood.

Product and development managers already spent about a fifth of their time on design. Thirteen spent more than half, and scored around three times the average maturity. With a matrix of every product's stage, revenue, and receptiveness, that found the first cohort of twenty.

Manager interviews said what the cohort needed: how to write a testable hypothesis, design an experiment, and recruit users. So the programme was blueprinted like an internal startup, with the five measures a startup would track, written before the first cohort so it could fail visibly rather than quietly.

LUMA Institute would deliver two of the three training days and the playbook. Userlytics records and redacts protected health information, which let studies run under HIPAA. Glean.ly's alpha programme gave us the repository. A pilot of six was set for September 2019 and the first cohort of twenty for October, then one a quarter toward a hundred practitioners leading research.

A manager had once said Inovalon was not the kind of place designers would want to work. The postings drew more than 200 qualified applicants in two months. I left in July 2019 with the core team hired and the first cohort on the calendar. A hundred birds, and the route left to them.

Afterwards

What it changed

  • The headcount was real. The AVP of Design Strategy role was filled, and the function carried on under the people who had sponsored it. By 2021 they reported fifty-plus leaders trained and a design system across thirty-five-plus products.
  • My manager changed careers. The senior director I reported to, new to human-centred design in 2018, moved into design full time in 2020 and led design strategy there until 2021.
  • A colleague became a client. A director from Inovalon's innovation group left in 2018 to found an AI text-analytics company. In 2023 that company hired me to design its brand and product interface.
  • The vendor's rates stopped being the ceiling. The budget workbook, not a slide, became the way design was funded: change an assumption, see the total.
