A city's open-data portal, and the people it wasn't built for.

In 2017 the City of Philadelphia's open-data portal was a catalog of datasets. Its open-data team suspected that the people who could use them most were the ones who could not. As a design fellow I had nine weeks to find out who used the portal, and what stopped everyone else.

I held ten talks with the City's data staff and outside experts. I ran fifteen interviews with community leaders and analysts, six of them at the portal itself, and put a pop-up survey on the site. Two personas came out of it, on two axes: civic engagement and digital literacy. So did a strategy report with six recommendations, presented at a press conference at the end of the fellowship.

Outline

Nine weeks to test a suspicion about open data, in a city with a digital divide, and to leave the open-data team a report on the people its portal was not built for.

{% include study-outline.html %}
{% include study-act.html n=1 %}

The City of Philadelphia publishes open data through a portal. It catalogs datasets from the City and other public bodies. The open-data team ran the program: awareness, custom charts and maps, hackathons, and the technical work of getting data out of departments. Most of what it published was spatial.

The team had a suspicion it wanted tested. Using open data took a high level of technical skill, in a city with a wide digital divide. So it hired two fellows that spring, a design fellow and a data-engineering fellow. The design fellowship got one question: how might we create a portal that makes open data more accessible to non-technical audiences? A second aim was to learn what the program was worth and what stood in its way.

I started on 2 May. In the onboarding, the Chief Data Officer framed the problem his way. Open data's advocates are vocal and its audience is narrow. Government does open data for transparency's sake, which he called an "eat your vegetables" premise, and that can only go so far. The aim was to show that the public depends on open data. Title companies depend on lien data, civic groups on zoning appeals, town-watch groups on crime data, journalists on the verdicts of assessment appeals. Better still, they should ask for what is missing.

I wrote the research plan in the first week. It began with a reading list of eleven documents and a look at eleven comparable products. Then came ten talks with the City's data staff and outside experts.

From 22 May to 9 June, three streams ran side by side. A five-question survey went out to open-data users. It traveled through other cities' chief data officers, a national network of civic analysts and the local open-data mailing list. A pop-up survey ran on the portal itself, for visitors who had stayed at least the average visit. It asked one thing: if you could change anything on this page, what would you have us do? And I ran fifteen recorded interviews of 30 to 60 minutes, over video or in person. I recruited by referral, a tweet, a post on the City's social pages and a personal note.

Seven of the ten talks in May were inside the City. The data services manager and the Chief Data Officer. A former chief data officer and the chief geographic information officer. A data engineer, a communications intern and an innovation program manager. Three talks were outside the City: the head of a local geospatial software company, a journalism educator and a university center for government excellence. Two questions framed them all. What is the open-data program, and why is it valuable? What challenges does it face?

I clustered the notes on a digital whiteboard, value on the left, challenges on the right. Five findings came out.

A portal's job is to catalog current open data in a form people can use. Portals are built for analysts and programmers to find and download datasets. Metadata links one dataset to another. Inside city government, managers and analysts use other departments' data to plan programs. One example: overlaying datasets to decide which buildings at risk of collapse to inspect first.

Open data builds trust through transparency. Reporters use it to write stories that carry weight, without filing right-to-know requests, the state's public-records requests, which can take up to two months. Open data also makes the public a partner. Nonprofits write grants with it, elected officials use it, civic hackers build on it, and academics start research from it.

The community groups best placed to serve the public with open data often lack the data skills to do so. Portals were built for a technical audience and assume one. The challenge side was the cost. Releasing and maintaining a dataset means privacy law, scattered and outdated systems, cleaning, metadata, and a queue with no clear way to rank it.

From 29 May to 9 June I interviewed fifteen people who used open data or should have been able to. Eight were community leaders: advocates, journalists and people who run registered community groups. Seven were data analysts: academics, researchers and software developers. Six of them then walked through the existing portal, saying what they were thinking as they went. The pop-up survey and the questionnaire ran the same weeks. I read the web analytics beside them, and all of it went into one synthesis. Four findings came out.

First, most of them used data to organize volunteers or donors around a cause in their neighborhood. Often it was something reported to 311, the City's service-request line. A request for open data usually starts with a problem seen on the ground.

Second, people could say what data was worth to them. But they expected to query for points of interest, not datasets. The less familiar ones typed data points straight into the search bar, "thefts 19123", and got sent the wrong way. Large datasets in unfamiliar file types overwhelmed them. They could not tell which dataset held the point they wanted. Whatever their skill, they said they would look for a map.

Third, tracking down data can take months, and it depends on who you know. Three community leaders said their schedules left no time to hunt through datasets. One participant said reporters skip the portal and file a right-to-know request when they need the freshest data. That was the very thing the experts had said open data spared them.

Fourth, people spent their own time enriching and collecting data, but rarely shared it back. Analysts deleted columns, reformatted fields and fixed typos before every use. Three leaders of community groups were collecting data they wanted to share with other groups. Two used crowdsourcing to gather it. One architect wished he could send corrections back to the City.

The fifteen came from many professions. Yet two things explained how they worked with open data better than their job did: civic engagement and digital literacy. A person who knows a neighborhood well but has no data experience gives up at the first cleaning and joining. A person with strong data skills may not know a community's problems, or who is organized around them.

I wrote two personas into the report, one at the high end of each axis. The Data Analyst has high technical skill and works from a desktop. Urban planners, architects, civil engineers, researchers, data journalists and developers. They want to find patterns and insights worth sharing. To get there they must find, fix, clean and join datasets first. Missing metadata stalls them, and with few connections they struggle to track down what is not published.

The Community Leader speaks for a neighborhood. They have wide connections but less technical skill, and they are mobile and not always online: advocates, organizers, journalists, nonprofit leaders, grant writers and volunteers. They collect data other groups would find useful, and they want city-wide pictures of need. They use open data to find funding. Raw data overwhelms them, and they are short on time.

I drafted a journey map across ten phases, from discovering a problem to following it. At each phase I wrote what a person does and what hurts.

Then I ran an ideation workshop with the open-data team. I turned the findings into eight prompts, each a question that starts with how might we. Each used a different technique. Amp up the good. Remove the bad. Explore the opposite. Question an assumption. Go after the adjectives. Find an unexpected resource. Make an analogy. Play against the challenge. The team put up thirty ideas in four clusters of 10, 12, 5 and 3.

The prompts chased the analysts' pain, cleaning and joining. The two ideas the team marked strongest answered the community leaders instead. Crowdsource the curation of data charts and maps. And give advocates and analysts a space to connect and self-organize. Draft concepts went to the team in mid-June and were revised on its feedback.

The strategy report is dated 28 June 2017. Its value proposition was simple. Serve anyone on the spectrum of data literacy and civic engagement, and the portal serves analysts and community leaders alike. Analysts need to find and link datasets, judge their quality and download them. Community leaders expect map tools that show the points they care about. Both need a way to request new data from anyone who might have it.

The report makes six recommendations. The readout showed each beside a comparable product that already existed, since we had built nothing yet.

Put the map first, so the first encounter with data is a layer over the environment, and list and detail views come second. Let users add map layers and subscribe to updates. Then people can request data without knowing which dataset holds it, and demand shows up as trends. Let users add to the public pool of open data beyond any regional limit, through a review-and-merge flow like the one developers use for code, which keeps protected data out.

Let users create profiles, lend their authority to data and share insights about it, so data's original author is visible and trust has a basis. Help users document, clean and link data with shared vocabularies that grow with use, rather than a standards body, the way the linked-data web already does. That effort can then improve search. Help users judge data quality on six counts: completeness, consistency, uniqueness, validity, accuracy and timeliness. Alert the people likely to know about a missing field.

The report's next step was measurement before building. Define the changes in public behavior the team wants to see. Gather baselines for civic engagement, data use, requests and contributions. Only then build prototypes against them. After that, research the residents with low data or digital literacy, and the businesses that use open data. Bring in community advisors, departments and the wider civic-analytics network.

At the end of the fellowship I presented the findings at a press conference. On 6 July the City published an article on the two fellows' work, quoting me: "The datasets themselves are just tools that can be used in many different ways. In order to get anything done you need more than a bag of hammers. You need people, you need reasons, you need a problem that you want to solve." Read the City's article, 6 July 2017 →.

Afterwards

{{ page.after }}

The fellowship delivered research and a strategy. The screens came later, on my own time, to make the results easier to share. What the City built from the report I do not know. The fellowship ended in July and the recommendations stayed with the team.

In 2018 I drew an interaction graph. Two kinds of user, the analyst and the community leader, joined by twelve actions to four kinds of thing: sources, insights, initiatives and open data. Each thing holds the attributes the portal would need, the six quality dimensions among them.

In 2020, for a short series of posts, I drew five phone screens of the portal the report described. A city map with a pin. A list of layers, with the count of sources behind each. The sources behind a layer, each with a trust button. A spreadsheet published as a new layer. A hazard photographed, tagged and published from the sidewalk. Four of the five are redrawn below.

The same series regrouped the four findings into three systemic challenges. Redundant use of energy: the same data requested and cleaned over and over. Incomplete knowledge loops: community leaders acting on data without coordination. And data that is not findable, accessible, interoperable or reusable, a framing borrowed from the FAIR data principles for research data.

The City's article put the job in one line: to empower networks of people by finding ways to make browsing and sharing data more intuitive.
